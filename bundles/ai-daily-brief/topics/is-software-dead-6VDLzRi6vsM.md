# Overview

Nathaniel Whittemore takes on the question driving a full-blown market panic: is software dead? SaaS stocks have cratered — Salesforce down 21% on the year, Snowflake 23%, HubSpot 36%, AppLovin 37% — in what a Jefferies trader dubbed "the SaaS apocalypse," with "get me out" style selling accelerating over days. Unlike the dozen AI-related sell-offs since ChatGPT's release, this is the first time the market has tried to price in broad-based disruption that could kill an entire sector, extending to gaming stocks (Unity down 35%, Take-Two down 39% after Google's Genie 3) and, in the tipping-point moment, data-provider stocks tumbling on a Claude Cowork legal plugin.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]

Whittemore surveys both sides: vibe-coding anecdotes (CNBC's Deirdre Bosa rebuilding Monday.com in an hour; his own head of sales replacing the company website) versus the counterargument that large enterprises run on decades of layered systems moved by risk tolerance, not expectations — plus Jensen Huang's "would the ultimate AI invent a new screwdriver or just use one?" and Klarna's Sebastian Siemiatkowski conceding his Salesforce-replacement experiment isn't a template for most companies. His conclusion: "is X dead" hyperbole is always wrong, but so is complacency — the real question is how software changes, who loses, and who benefits.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]

# Key points

- Market context: Apollo co-president John Zito called "is software dead?" the real risk (ahead of tariffs, inflation, rates); Apollo had already cut software exposure in private credit funds from 20% to 10% and shorted names; Lightspeed's Isaac Kim declared "technology private equity in its current form is dead" because AI breaks the assumption that products stay relevant long enough for financial engineering to work.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]
- The bear framework ("the great SaaS meltdown"): growth-at-all-costs is dead, AI threatens both growth durability (agents replacing core functions) and margins (inference costs), and per-seat pricing faces an existential "seat crisis" — why pay for 100 seats when AI lets 10 people do the work.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]
- Disruption anecdotes are spreading beyond early adopters: a non-technical prospect built internal sales workflows in Replit to replace paid SaaS; YC's Chris Pasarski warns people aren't taking it seriously enough.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]
- Counterpoints: enterprises run on ERP, mainframes, and fragile integrations no one touches without 12-month change plans — "stocks move on expectations; enterprise architecture moves on risk tolerance"; Jensen Huang called the replace-software thesis "the most illogical thing in the world"; Klarna's Siemiatkowski, a year into replacing Salesforce, wrote "I don't think it's the end of Salesforce. It might be the opposite," predicting fewer, consolidated SaaS vendors.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]
- The WSJ's Dan Gallagher argues AI won't kill software, just its growth story: vendors must disprove a negative amid tightening corporate spending, layoffs shrinking seat counts, and internal AI projects consuming IT budgets — at minimum giving customers leverage in renewals; Ben Thompson calls the industry-wide rerating "completely justified" since businesses want to cut software spend to fund their own tokens.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]
- Chetan Wang's intuition: AI makes strong software companies stronger and weak ones weaker, because strong moats were never software but distribution, proprietary data, workflow integration, lock-in, network effects, and compliance — weak companies' moat "was just software."[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]
- Gokul Rajaram's rejoinder to Huang: when AI agents choose the tools, tool selection is optimized continuously — "the very definition of commoditization" — which is worse for software companies, not better.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]
- Optimist takes: YC's Gary Tan — "SaaS without agents may suffer but agent SaaS is alive, well and winning"; John Lober — incumbents extracting value from market position may finally have to compete on quality, so "maybe we will finally have good software."[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]
- OpenClaw creator Peter Steinberger's claim that AI will replace 80% of phone apps strikes Whittemore as more plausible than a 50,000-person industrial giant vibe-coding its own Workday.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]

# Takeaways

Whittemore's base case is enormous disruption whose exact categories are hard to call. He invokes comparative advantage to argue every company won't become an everything company, but expects massive implications for how software is priced, contract leverage, and procurement. He is "almost for sure" that in a decade we'll use ten times as much software as today — while having no idea what that means for any individual vendor, since the landscape could look dramatically different. The sell-off may prove a DeepSeek-style overblown blip, warranting calm about the pace of change, but he finds the market "accidentally behaving in a healthy manner": the rerating of SaaS might ultimately be healthy even if the immediate reaction is too strong.[^is-software-dead-6VDLzRi6vsM]

# Overview

In this weekend big-think episode, Nathaniel Whittemore engages at length with David Brooks' Atlantic essay "The People Who Will Thrive in the AI Age," which argues that what differentiates people won't be intelligence but their relationship to mental effort — sorting workers into "productive passengers" (low need for cognition, at risk of capability atrophy), "reluctant optimizers" (well-intentioned but sucked into over-reliance), and "mental marathoners" (who wrestle with AI to expand their capacities). The backdrop is a Section AI proficiency report finding that while 69% of workers say their organization has acted on AI agents, only 16% actually use an agentic tool at work, fewer than 10% can define an AI agent, and only 30% at agent-adopting organizations have received agentic training.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]

Whittemore pushes back on Brooks' implicit fatalism: he thinks Brooks misses the biggest opportunity — using AI not for things you can already do, but for things you can't — and disagrees that only the marathoners survive. The episode closes with two models for institutionalizing that stretch: the "AI champions" role documented by the Wall Street Journal, and Uber's "agentic pods" program pairing AI-proficient engineers with business-domain experts.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]

# Key points

- Brooks cites ActivTrak research on 10,000+ workers: AI adoption made work more intense, not less — email/messaging time more than doubled, business-software use rose 94%, focused uninterrupted work fell 9% — a state dubbed "AI brain fry"; Midjourney founder David Holz echoed feeling "extremely productive and also extremely drained."[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- Whittemore connects this to his "infinite backlog" concept: since agents don't need weekends or sleep, the limit has shifted from how much we can do to how much planning and oversight we can support.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- Brooks' guiding principle: "When intelligence is plentiful, volition is valuable" — and need for cognition correlates with but isn't the same as intelligence.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- Cited cognitive-decline evidence: MIT Media Lab found brain connectivity declines up to 55% when using ChatGPT for similar tasks, and a Possibility Sciences study found gamma-wave activity (a sign of cognitive effort) dropped ~40% with AI use; in a GoTo survey, 43% of workers admitted submitting AI-generated content they suspected contained errors.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- Brooks' mental-marathoner tactics include asking AI to challenge your analysis rather than produce it, and letting AI write functional emails but not essays; Whittemore counters that shaming AI reliance for rote corporate writing misjudges the value of different types of work.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- Whittemore's alternative: the people whose brains are "lighting up" are those doing previously impossible things — like non-coders building agents through humbling trial-and-error — because mental elasticity comes from doing uncomfortable, unfamiliar things, and AI has raised the ambition level of what to try next.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- The WSJ's "AI champions" article describes super-fans who get early tool access and training in exchange for converting skeptics; Whittemore argues their real value isn't internal PR but showing colleagues what they could actually be doing — akin to his predicted "internally deployed vibe coders" role.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- Uber CTO Praveen Napali's numbers: 99% of Uber engineers use AI tools, 70%+ of pull requests are attributed to agents, and engineers have built 2,500+ agent skills.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- Uber's "agentic pods": ~30 AI-proficient engineers paired with business-domain experts on two-week sprints (shadow, prioritize, build, validate, ship); 16 pods in two months produced results like capital allocation across 150 cities going from 15 hours to 30 minutes, financial pacing reports from two days to 10 minutes, and marketing web QA from two weeks to 50 minutes.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]
- Uber's biggest lesson: "the workflow becomes the unit of automation, not the individual task," and the best opportunities are discovered "by sitting next to the people doing the work... building with them, not for them."[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]

# Takeaways

Whittemore's central disagreement with Brooks: intrinsic need for cognition is not destiny. He argues education and work have simply failed to ask much of people for a long time — "we haven't stretched them, we haven't challenged them" — and that if AI adoption is done well, with genuine support, "we will find far more potential in people to be maximized than most people realize is there." On Uber's pods, his deeper prediction is that the lasting value isn't the engineers' two-week optimizations but what happens over months as business people, influenced by agentic working patterns, rethink their work at a core level — the real transformation comes from reinvesting productivity gains into new, previously impossible work, not doing more of the same.[^how-to-help-people-thrive-with-ai-bl76qV0xwec]

# Overview

Whittemore digs into an ongoing study by Berkeley Haas professors Aruna Ranganathan and Shixi Maggie Ye, published in the Harvard Business Review, in which the researchers embedded with a 200-employee technology company from April to December of the prior year. The finding: in lived context, AI is not reducing work — it is increasing and intensifying it, through three mechanisms the researchers call task expansion, blurred boundaries between work and non-work, and more multitasking. Whittemore frames this as neither good nor bad a priori, but as finally letting us respond to what's actually happening rather than what we predicted.[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]

He connects the study to the agentic shift he's tracked all year — the "Claude Code / Opus 4.5 / Codex 5.3 moment" — where the dominant feeling has flipped from "am I valuable?" to "am I doing enough?", and to an Anthropic agentic coding trends report predicting coding capabilities democratize beyond engineering and single agents evolve into coordinated teams.[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]

# Key points

- Task expansion: because AI fills knowledge gaps, product managers and designers began writing code, researchers took on engineering tasks, and people attempted work they'd previously have outsourced, deferred, or avoided — experiments that "accumulated into a meaningful widening of job scope" with an "empowering cognitive boost."[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]
- Blurred boundaries: because starting a task is so easy, workers slipped prompts into lunch, meetings, and loading screens, and sent "a quick last prompt" before leaving so AI could work while they stepped away.[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]
- More multitasking: manually writing code while AI generated an alternative version, running multiple agents in parallel, and reviving long-deferred tasks in the background.[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]
- Spillover costs: engineers spent more time reviewing, correcting, and coaching colleagues' vibe-coded work and "finishing partially complete pull requests."[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]
- The frog-boiling effect: workers realized only in hindsight that habitual break-time prompting eroded recovery — work became "less bounded and more ambient, something that could always be advanced a little further" — while speed expectations rose implicitly through what became visible and normalized.[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]
- The researchers suggest management responses: intentional pauses, sequencing, and human grounding.[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]
- Corroborating voices: Greg Brockman ("Feels like such a wasted opportunity every moment your agents aren't running") and Simon Willison, who wrote that the productivity boost "is exhausting" — a couple of hours of parallel projects depletes his mental energy for the day, and people are losing sleep to "just one more prompt."[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]
- Anthropic's agentic coding trends report: trend seven predicts non-technical use cases expand across organizations with domain experts implementing solutions directly; trend five expects agentic coding to reach new services and users (NLW's 2026 prediction of internally deployed vibe coders, previewed by Lovable's full-time vibe coder Lazar Jovanovic on Lenny's Podcast); trend two predicts multi-agent systems replace single-agent workflows — already visible in the OpenClaw mission-control craze.[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]
- NLW's summary of the moment: "all of us are now managers, and we are all feeling the sting of a big, highly capable team that's being underutilized because we haven't gotten it together to tell them what to do."[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]

# Takeaways

Whittemore's biggest takeaway — one the authors don't discuss — is that the study reminds us the aggregate amount of work is not fixed: it expands to accommodate capacity, which changes the long-term calculus on AI job disruption. He predicts short-term cost-cutters will be rewarded briefly by markets, but the winning organizations will treat AI as an expansionary, opportunity-creating technology — "doing more with the same, or way more with a little more" via new product lines, revenue streams, and markets — a view he sees reinforced by the "SaaS-pocalypse" punishing companies that merely maintain trajectories. He accepts the researchers' warning that the new intensification requires new organizational structures, and welcomes being able to respond to reality rather than prediction.[^why-ai-leads-to-more-work-not-less-owFqgMcmDbA]

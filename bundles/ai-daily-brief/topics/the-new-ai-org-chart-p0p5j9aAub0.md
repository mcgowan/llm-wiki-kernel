# Overview

In this long-reads episode, Nathaniel Whittemore examines how AI agents are changing not just individual work but the fundamental shape of organizations, through two contrasting case studies. The first is an essay by Jack Dorsey (fresh off Block's 40% layoffs) and Sequoia partner Roelof Botha, which traces hierarchy from the Roman contubernium through Prussian general staff, railroad org charts, Taylorism, and the matrix corporation — arguing hierarchy has always been an information-routing protocol built around the human span-of-control limit, and that AI is the first technology capable of performing that coordination function directly. Block intends to rebuild itself as "an intelligence or mini AGI" around four elements: capabilities, a company-plus-customer world model, an intelligence layer, and interfaces — normalizing humans down to three roles and eliminating permanent middle management.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]

The second is Every's lived, bottom-up version from Dan Shipper's AI & I podcast: a company "half agent now," where each person's personal agent organically specialized into a parallel shadow org chart. Whittemore compares the clean top-down architecture against the messy trench reality — agents that can't shut up in group chats, imagination as the real capability gap — finding both converge on one point: the information-routing manager is what AI replaces first.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]

# Key points

- Dorsey and Botha's historical arc: the Roman 8-80-480-5,000 nested hierarchy encoded a span-of-control limit of three to eight people; Prussia's post-Jena general staff invented middle management; West Point engineers brought military structure to 1850s railroads, where Daniel McCallum drew the first org chart; Taylor, McKinsey's matrix, and the 7S framework refined but never escaped the trade-off.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- Flat-structure experiments (Spotify squads, Zappos holacracy, Valve) all reverted or failed to scale because no alternative information-routing mechanism existed — "AI is that technology."[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- Block's foundation is two world models: a company world model built from remote-first, machine-readable work artifacts, and a customer world model built from "the most honest signal in the world" — money — seeing both sides of millions of daily transactions via Cash App and Square.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- The intelligence layer composes atomic financial capabilities (payments, lending, banking, payroll) into proactive solutions — e.g., surfacing a short-term loan to a restaurant before a seasonal cash-flow dip; when composition fails for lack of a capability, that failure signal becomes the roadmap, replacing product-manager hypotheses.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- Block normalizes to three roles: individual contributors (deep specialists per layer), DRIs owning cross-cutting problems with authority to pull resources (e.g., merchant churn in a segment for 90 days), and player coaches handling craft and people — no permanent middle-management layer.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- Dorsey's test question for every company: "What does your company understand that is genuinely hard to understand, and is that understanding getting deeper every day?" If nothing, AI is just cost optimization before absorption "by something smarter."[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- At Every, a parallel org chart emerged un-designed: growth lead Austin's agent Montaigne became the growth expert, Dan's R2C2 the product bot — "compound engineering," thousands of micro-interactions distilling each person's philosophy into their agent.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- Personal ownership is Every's trust layer: "Claude belongs to everyone, A+1 belongs to you" — reputational skin in the game means Austin implicitly stands behind Montaigne's MRR numbers, something corporate AI governance can't replicate.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- The "Midjourney effect": agents working publicly in shared channels transmit trust and capability awareness across the org — but only in closed, trusted communities.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- Failure modes: the "ant death spiral," where agents in group channels trigger each other in infinite loops burning millions of tokens; models aren't trained for group-chat dynamics, and boss-agent gatekeepers double compute costs.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- The capability gap is imagination, not technology: Brandon's agent could already make voice calls for weeks before a missing city bike prompted him to have it call and walk through his inbox — building the delegation instinct is the hardest adoption problem.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]
- Unsolved: how skills learned by one agent propagate across 20, 200, or 2,000 agents — an organizational rather than technical problem.[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]

# Takeaways

Whittemore's synthesis: Block is designing top-down toward a centralized world model that replaces middle management entirely, while Every stumbled bottom-up into distributed intelligence where trust flows from personal ownership — Dan's "Claude is everybody's, A+1 is mine" reads almost as a rebuttal of the centralized thesis, though Whittemore suspects the approaches may converge over time. Every's trench experience exposes practical gaps Dorsey's theoretical essay skips, and Dorsey's three-role model assumes a reliability the intelligence layer doesn't yet have. But both case studies agree on the core: the classic middle-management function of aggregating information up and relaying decisions down is the first thing AI replaces. "I don't think anyone knows exactly how the org chart changes, but we can be confident that it will."[^the-new-ai-org-chart-p0p5j9aAub0]

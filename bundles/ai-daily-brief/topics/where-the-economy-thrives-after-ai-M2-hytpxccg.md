# Overview

In this weekend episode, Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW) vents his "major beef" with the AI jobs discourse — he argues the AI labs pitch AI backwards compared to pharma ads (leading with harms instead of benefits), and that predictions of 15-50% unemployment are "just straight-up wrong" because almost no one spends time modeling what the economy looks like after AI and agents are fully integrated. His own framing is that AI shifts the economy's binding constraint from supply to demand and consumption capacity, with healthcare as his example of a sector where people would consume vastly more services if AI-driven cost decreases made preventative care and monitoring affordable.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]

The bulk of the episode is a read-through of economist Alex Imas's viral essay "What Will Be Scarce: The Economics of Structural Change at the Post-Commodity Future of Work." Imas argues that even if AI can replicate most commodity production, human labor does not disappear: as people get richer, demand shifts toward "relational" goods and services whose value is inseparable from the human who provided them, triggering a sectoral reallocation like the historical move from farms to factories to services.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]

# Key points

- Imas told Fortune his first reaction to AI was fear, and he worked through history and preferences "in order to be less scared" — not from an ideological pro-AI stance.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- Starbucks ($112B market cap) is Imas's canary-in-the-coal-mine example: after automating stores, CEO Brian Nichols reversed course — handwritten notes on cups, ceramic cups, and seating drove satisfaction, and more baristas per store are being hired while automation is rolled back.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- The essay builds on David Autor and Neil Thompson's framework: automating simple tasks makes jobs more specialized (wages rise), automating hard tasks makes jobs more accessible (employment expands, wages fall) — same technology, opposite outcomes.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- Historical structural change: farming employed about 40% of the American workforce in 1900 versus less than 2% today, yet food production boomed — labor reallocated to manufacturing and then services.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- A 2021 Econometrica paper by Comin, Lashkari, and Mestieri estimates income effects account for over 75% of observed structural change; as people get richer they shift spending to high-income-elasticity sectors, with Baumol's cost disease amplifying the shift.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- In the 2022 BLS consumer expenditure study, top-quintile households spent about 4.3x the bottom quintile overall, but far more in relational categories like in-person dining, entertainment, and education.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- Drawing on René Girard's mimetic desire, Imas argues comparative, status-driven wants are hard to satiate and therefore highly income elastic; in experiments with Greylin Mandel, human-made artwork gained 44% in value from exclusivity versus only 21% for AI-generated work — AI involvement makes goods feel inherently reproducible.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- The relational sector extends beyond art to teachers, nurses, therapists, child care workers, hospitality, clergy, and guides — cases where human judgment, warmth, or presence is integral to the value.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- Imas frames Baumol's cost disease as "a feature, not a bug" under AI: the sector that resists automation becomes relatively expensive precisely as it absorbs growing spending and employment.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- Durable future jobs per Imas: not AI monitoring or prompt engineering (transitional roles) but relational work — plus emerging roles like experience designers, human-AI collaboration artists, provenance certifiers, and community curators; six of ten jobs today didn't exist in 1940.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]
- Imas cautions the framework fits the developed world best; for developing economies built on commodity exports the picture is "more complicated and potentially more worrying."[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]

# Takeaways

Whittemore's conclusion is that the dominant doomer discourse — assuming a "labor cliff" where you either work at an AI lab or join a "permanent underclass" — is itself fueling political backlash, because people reasonably ask why they should permit an obviously negative change. He predicts that in one to three decades this moment of "frenetic anxiety" will look like one of the biggest misplacements of collective energy ever, and he welcomes Imas's essay as bringing intellectual and academic discipline to the question of what the economy changes into, a theme he plans to keep exploring on the show.[^where-the-economy-thrives-after-ai-M2-hytpxccg]

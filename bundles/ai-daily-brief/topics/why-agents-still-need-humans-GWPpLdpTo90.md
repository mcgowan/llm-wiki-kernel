# Overview

In this long-read episode, Whittemore builds on his "infinite backlog" thesis (from "Why Agents Make Every Job a Startup") to explore the next wave of human-agent collaboration. The centerpiece is Every CEO Dan Shipper's essay "After Automation," which starts from a paradox: Every has automated everything it can — Codex and Claude Code across coding, writing, design, and customer service; AI answering 95% of Shipper's work emails — yet the roughly 30-person company has "more human work to do than ever." Shipper's explanation: AI commoditizes "the residue of human expertise," collapsing the value of default model output and creating demand for what's different — which is demand for human experts, even approaching AGI.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]

Whittemore layers on evidence that work patterns are maturing: Every abandoned one-personal-agent-per-employee in favor of shared team agents; OpenClaw's mega-autonomy Mac mini pattern is giving way to semi-synchronous harness workflows in Codex and Claude Code; and markets may be starting to reward AI-driven growth over AI-driven layoffs.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]

# Key points

- Shipper catalogs the doom consensus he's pushing against: Dario Amodei warning AI could wipe out up to half of entry-level white-collar jobs; Meta laying off 8,000 while installing keystroke/mouse-capture software to gather knowledge-work training data; Ken Griffin saying "extraordinarily high-skilled jobs" are being automated.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- Shipper's counter: "There's no tipping point coming where things flip and the jobs are gone... The more we automate, the more expert human work there is to do."[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- Two modes of agent work at Every: "agents as employees" (Slack co-worker agents like Andy, which compiles editorial nuggets into newsletter digests, and embedded agents like Finn handling customer support) versus the "stranger and more important" mode — human-agent collaboration in Codex, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork as "operating systems for the work itself."[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- The "human sandwich" pattern: humans set the frame and define good on one side, AI collapses the task into drafts/searches/code in the middle, and humans judge and extend on the other side.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- Every's pivot from personal replica agents to team agents: personal agents broke and each owner had to fix their own; a shared analytics agent means one skills update benefits the whole team, and team agents retain company context when employees leave.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- Shipper's feedback loop: AI makes yesterday's competence cheap → cheap competence gets rapidly adopted (ops people issuing pull requests, marketers making thumbnails) → abundance creates sameness ("slop is visible sameness repeated ad nauseam") → sameness creates demand for difference → demand for difference is demand for human experts.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- Matt Shumer wiped his OpenClaw Mac mini to turn it into an always-on dev box for Codex mobile; NLW notes OpenClaw-style heartbeat autonomy mostly excelled at "burning tokens" — a real cost in the token-shortage era.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- OpenAI's Nick Bowman described a three-device Codex setup (phone, MacBook, always-on Mac mini) with threads resumable anywhere — "the shape of it feels very real to me" as a near-future work pattern.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- Gartner argues that even with short-term AI layoffs beginning in 2028, AI will create more jobs than it eliminates.[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]
- Market signal: Atlassian's 10% March layoffs preceded a year-to-date low, but 29% Q1 earnings growth anchored on AI-enhanced product sales sent the stock up 29% in an evening; Dan Ives warns tech companies against "talking about job cuts not reading the room" and says what separates companies is people, since "LLMs are going to get commodified."[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]

# Takeaways

Whittemore's recommendations: personally, reduce latency with steering and voice input to work semi-synchronously with agents rather than turn-based; organizationally, map the Venn-diagram overlap of people's jobs and build shared agents in that space, where AI-native companies like Every are finding their value. His bigger claim is that agents' net impact on employment will be expansion "in proportion to all of the new things that we can do now that we never could have before," and that winning companies will invest in team capability with agents, prioritize growth over efficiency, and treat agents "not as a get-out-of-budget-jail-free card, but one of the best investment opportunities that companies have ever had." The next wave of human-agent collaboration, he concludes, "is going to be a good one."[^why-agents-still-need-humans-GWPpLdpTo90]

# Overview

Nathaniel Whittemore traces the "zero-human company" from its precursor — the tiny-teams movement documented by Shawn "Swyx" Wang and Henry Shih's Lean AI Leaderboard, both measured by revenue per employee — to a wave of post-Open Claw experiments that dispense with human operators entirely. He walks through three examples: Nat Eliason's Felix Craft, an autonomous business roughly 30 days old; Pulsia, a platform for spinning up autonomous companies; and Tom Osman's ZHC.company, alongside newer entrants like Yoshizen, Gauntlet's Kelly, and the factoryfloor.dev leaderboard.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]

Whittemore's assessment is split. He credits Pulsia founder Ben's method — skip to the end state where AI can do everything and see what breaks — as capturing the real logic of the moment, and argues the experiments will teach everyone about agent capabilities regardless of whether the companies succeed. But he remains skeptical that mechanically executing startup activities produces market success, and identifies human attention as the binding constraint the model fails to account for.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]

# Key points

- Swyx defined tiny teams as having more millions in ARR than employees; across seven teams totaling ~100 people and $200M ARR, he found paid work trials, product-led hiring, top-of-market salaries, senior generalists, an AI chief of staff, and almost no meetings.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]
- Henry Shih, formerly of Super.com and now at Anthropic, runs a Lean AI Leaderboard tracking revenue per employee; Midjourney, Surge, and Cursor rank near the top, with single-digit-headcount companies further down still earning millions.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]
- Felix Craft, built by Nat Eliason, generated just under $78,000 in its first ~30 days, $40,000 of it in the final seven, across four revenue streams shown on a public dashboard.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]
- Felix's biggest earner (~$41,000) is a $29 guidebook on how to hire an AI, written entirely by the agent; Clawmark, an "app store for AI assistants," contributed ~$25,000 plus ~$11,000 in marketplace cuts, selling AI personas ($49 for a content-marketing agent, $99 for a Felix template) and mostly-free skills. Polyscript, a collaborative writing platform, has earned only $230.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]
- Pulsia, built by Ben (Sarah/Broca) starting in November and completed in about a month, generates a business idea, mission statement, market research, homepage, and background tasks; a $49/month subscription buys 30 days of autonomy, 45 total agent tasks, a web server, database, email address, and $5/month of API credits.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]
- Pulsia jumped from low single-digit thousands in ARR at the start of February to a $1.5M run rate, gaining $1M in a single week, with over 1,500 active companies; Ben frames the subscription as cost recovery and the real model as a 20% revenue share — "think incubator, not SaaS."[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]
- Pulsia twice proposed the same business to Whittemore — "Headcount," a workforce management platform for AI employees with roles, KPIs, performance reviews, and org-chart visibility — which he judged a genuinely apt fit.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]
- Tom Osman and his agent co-founder Juno launched ZHC.company plus a paid Institute for Zero Human Companies; Whittemore notes a pattern where much of the realized early revenue comes from other people wanting to run similar experiments.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]
- Swyx pushed back on the framing, calling the focus on one person "kind of an ego trip" and arguing media bias favors hero narratives when "it takes a village to do anything consequential and reliable."[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]

# Takeaways

Whittemore argues the experiments are worth watching regardless of outcome, because what ZHC builders learn will inform everyone's agent-work strategies. On value creation he holds two opposing views: going through the motions of company-building guarantees nothing — most startups fail and most successes pivot — yet the collapsing cost of execution makes a counterargument, that reversing the flow and taking many more shots on goal could be a legitimate path. His deciding factor is human attention: even if 50 of Pulsia's 1,500 companies were perfect for him, he could never find them among the tweets, and attention is getting scarcer, not more abundant. He ties this to what he previously called the "work slop" problem — the gap between more output and more quality output, since outcomes, not input volume, determine business success. He closes by conceding he could be wrong and end up looking like Paul Krugman on the internet.[^the-rise-of-the-zero-human-company-at_Fng0Lbow]

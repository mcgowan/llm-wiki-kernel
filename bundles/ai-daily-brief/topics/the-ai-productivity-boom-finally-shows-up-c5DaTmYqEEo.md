# Overview

Nathaniel Whittemore examines whether AI's productivity impact is finally moving from anecdote to actual economic evidence. The centerpiece is Stanford economist Erik Brynjolfsson's Financial Times opinion piece arguing the US is in a massive AI productivity boom: the Bureau of Labor Statistics revised 2025 job creation down from 584,000 to just 181,000 (removing ~400,000 jobs), yet GDP stayed remarkably strong — provisionally 3.7% in Q4 (Atlanta Fed GDPNow at 5.4%) after 4.4% in Q3. Since macro productivity is GDP divided by workers, fewer workers plus strong output implies productivity growth of about 2.7% for the year, nearly double the past decade's average pace.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]

Whittemore frames this against the historical lag problem — Robert Solow's 1987 line that "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics" — and Brynjolfsson's own 2018 "productivity J-curve" paper on general purpose technologies (coincidentally abbreviated GPTs), which predicted productivity would be understated during the intangible-investment phase and overstated during the later harvest phase. Brynjolfsson now argues 2025 data suggests the US is transitioning from investment to harvest — from "an era of AI experimentation to one of utility." Whittemore balances this with skeptics like economist Guy Berger, who notes the revision mostly reflected government, mining, transportation, and manufacturing cuts rather than AI-exposed industries, and with mounting evidence of white-collar hiring weakness.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]

# Key points

- BLS revised 2025 net job gains from 584,000 down to 181,000, while provisional Q4 GDP growth stands at 3.7% (Atlanta Fed GDPNow: 5.4%) after 4.4% in Q3 — implying ~2.7% productivity growth per Brynjolfsson, nearly double the decade average.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]
- Andrew Yang, in "The End of the Office," predicted "the great disemboweling of white-collar jobs" after a family member had AI build in minutes a website that would have taken a firm days.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]
- Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok had posed the conundrum — "AI is everywhere except in the incoming macroeconomic data" — musing about a possible J-curve effect; Brynjolfsson argues the revision resolves it.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]
- Brynjolfsson's October 2018 J-curve paper argued GPTs require intangible complementary investments (processes, business models, human capital) that suppress measured productivity early and inflate it later.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]
- Noah Smith: "there's actually a huge productivity boom underway... It's AI"; University of Chicago's Alex Imas, who predicted gains would appear "sooner rather than later," remarked "I guess sooner came pretty quickly."[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]
- Economist Guy Berger called it "very thin evidence": the revision largely removed government workers (DOGE cuts) and layoffs in mining, logging, transportation, and manufacturing — not AI-exposed sectors.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]
- The Kobeissi Letter: professional and business services job openings are at 1.6 per 100 employees, an 11-year low, below the 2020 pandemic bottom; sector openings are down 1.4 million from the March 2022 peak, with the hiring rate at 4.2% — 2008-crisis levels.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]
- Politicians on both sides are engaging: Republican Jay Obernolte (who holds a master's in AI) says displacement is certain but historical evidence argues against permanent job loss, while calling for reskilling and a safety net; Senator Elizabeth Warren warns millions could lose jobs suddenly and "now is the moment when we need to be preparing."[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]
- Brynjolfsson's team's follow-up to "Canaries in the Coal Mine" found interest rates don't explain the disproportionate entry-level hiring decline in AI-exposed occupations, but conceded that with the broadest controls, the AI-linked employment decline becomes significant only in 2024 — earlier declines likely had mixed causes.[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]

# Takeaways

Whittemore's core point is methodological: as the disruption conversation gets more urgent, it must move from anecdotes and suppositions into actual evidence — and if Brynjolfsson is right that productivity gains are showing up already, that would break the historical pattern of decades-long lags and undercut the skeptics' strongest argument. He acknowledges no one fully understands what is happening yet and welcomes more granular research (like the study finding AI users spend more time on task, and Brookings work on worker adaptability). His closing position: whether you are pessimistic or optimistic, "change is not only coming, it is here. Navigating it well is going to take everything we've got."[^the-ai-productivity-boom-finally-shows-up-c5DaTmYqEEo]

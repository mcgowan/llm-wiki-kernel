# Overview

With AI job disruption a major theme at Davos and a likely election-year political issue, Whittemore walks through a new National Bureau of Economic Research study that argues exposure measures miss a key question: not just which jobs are susceptible to AI disruption, but how adaptable different workers are if displacement hits. The authors build a composite "adaptive capacity" index from four factors — liquid financial resources, age, geographic density, and skill transferability — across roughly 350 occupations covering about 96% of American employment, then cross it with an AI exposure index to sort workers into four quadrants.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]

The headline finding: highly AI-exposed workers are on average well equipped for transitions — software developers, financial managers, and lawyers (about 26.5 million workers) combine high exposure with strong pay, buffers, and networks — but 6.1 million workers face both high exposure and low adaptive capacity, concentrated in administrative and clerical jobs. Whittemore endorses the analysis as policy triage while critiquing its core assumption that displaced workers will have destination jobs to move into.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]

# Key points

- The four adaptive-capacity factors are grounded in prior studies: a 2008 study on liquid savings and post-job-loss distress; a 2017 study showing workers 55-64 were about 16 percentage points less likely than those 35-44 to find new work after Great Recession job loss; a 2012 study on denser areas easing transitions; and a 2016 study linking skill transferability to smaller earnings losses.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]
- Income and union representation were considered but excluded as less conclusively linked to outcomes; the index combines six primary data sets.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]
- About 26.5 million workers sit in the high-exposure/high-adaptive-capacity quadrant — professions "often cited as being highly threatened by AI" that likely possess strong means to adjust.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]
- The most concerning group is 6.1 million workers with high exposure and low adaptive capacity — administrative and clerical jobs with modest savings, limited skill transferability, and narrow reemployment prospects; 86% of these most vulnerable workers are women.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]
- Vulnerability clusters geographically in college towns and state capitals — Laramie (WY), Stillwater (OK), Springfield (IL), Carson City (NV) — where 5-7% of the local workforce falls in the high-vulnerability category.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]
- NLW's critique: every component of the index is calibrated in a world where displacement is localized and destination jobs exist — modeling AI as "a larger version of a plant closure or a trade shock" — whereas AI could pressure whole cognitive task categories simultaneously (secretary, customer service rep, claims processor, office clerk all exposed at once) so displaced workers can't absorb each other.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]
- He also notes skill transferability could become "a moving target" as AI radically revalues some skills and devalues others; the authors concede in passing that if AI "fundamentally reshapes the economy, these historical relationships may not hold."[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]
- The framework can compare a 58-year-old medical secretary in Springfield with $3,000 in savings to a 32-year-old Seattle developer with $200,000 in liquid assets — but not what happens if aggregate demand for human cognitive labor falls.[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]

# Takeaways

Whittemore argues the study remains useful precisely as "triage policy during a transition": even under total structural disruption, inertia will draw the change out, and the sequencing of disruption will likely follow the vulnerability gradient the research identifies. His honest reframing — "we don't know what the labor market looks like in 10 years, but we do know that these workers will face income disruption first, have the least capacity to self-insure, and are concentrated enough geographically to reach efficiently. We should get resources to them fast while we figure out the rest." He positions this as the kind of direct, discrete policy discussion he advocates over "fanciful imagine discussion."[^who-will-adapt-best-to-ai-disruption-GbxFFOAdn0Q]

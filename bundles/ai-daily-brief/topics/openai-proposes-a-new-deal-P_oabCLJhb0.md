# Overview

Whittemore examines OpenAI's policy document "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age," released at the convergence of two moments: lab signals that the next model jump is a big one (Anthropic's leaked "step change" Mythos post, OpenAI hyping its "Spud" model), and rapidly worsening American AI sentiment — a Quinnipiac poll shows 55% now believe AI will do more harm than good (up 11 points in a year) and Americans believe by 10-to-1 that AI will reduce rather than increase jobs, even as adoption climbs. He judges the document on two axes: as PR, and as policy substance.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]

His verdict is harsh on the first axis: the document lives in an "uncanny valley" — too technocratic to move public perception, too thin to advance actual policy. His deeper critique is of AI industry communications generally: no industry has been so unwilling to articulate why it deserves to exist, spending its airtime validating fears while hand-waving benefits. On substance, he walks through each proposal — worker voice, AI-first entrepreneurs, the right to AI, tax modernization, a public wealth fund, grid expansion, efficiency dividends, portable benefits, adaptive safety nets — endorsing some and doubting others, before landing on the most damning applied critique: OpenAI commits to nothing that would cost it anything.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]

# Key points

- Quinnipiac polling: 55% of Americans say AI will do more harm than good (up 11 points, a first-time majority); 70% believe AI will reduce job opportunities (up 14 points) versus just 7% who expect it to increase them; yet usage rose — researching topics went from 37% to 51%, and never-users fell from 33% to 27%.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
- Whittemore's drug-ad analogy: AI leaders flip the miracle-drug commercial ratio, spending three-quarters of the airtime on side effects and almost none on why the product should exist — leaving "because it makes some people rich" as the default public explanation.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
- On worker voice, he cites Will Manidis's essay "No New Deal for OpenAI": the document never uses the word "union," and the original New Deal came from decades of labor struggle, not benevolent capital-labor collaboration.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
- On the "right to AI," Whittemore argues access is meaningless without agency: companies spend more than 12 times as much on AI infrastructure as on people's capability to use the tools — he calls for a "Marshall Plan for education."[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
- On tax modernization he sees inevitability and "strange bedfellows": if the economy's balance shifts from labor to capital, taxation must shift commensurately — via capital gains hikes or automation taxes.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
- He is lukewarm on the public wealth fund — potentially "window-dressingy" because people want the exceptional, not the average — but enthusiastic about grid expansion that actively makes people's lives cheaper, and about efficiency dividends funding portable benefits, elder-care pathways, and retirement/healthcare support.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
- He flags adaptive safety nets — direct measurement of AI's impact on work and wages feeding automated, dynamic, targeted programs — as technocratic but worth much more conversation.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
- Manidis's core critique: OpenAI could pay the taxes it proposes, seed the wealth fund it proposes, accept voluntary rate separation, and reinstate the profit caps it dismantled six months earlier — instead the document offers "a workshop, fellowships paid in the company's own product, and an email address that routes to no one."[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
- Skeptics of the superintelligence framing: one commenter noted GPT-5.4 "spinning in circles on a webhook for 4 hours while Sam talks about superintelligence" — "we're in the extremely capable tool era, not the new social contract era"; Aaron Levie argued heavy agentic use reveals you probably need to hire more people, not fewer.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]

# Takeaways

Whittemore agrees wholeheartedly with OpenAI that these conversations are needed — on labor relations, taxation, redistribution of AI-generated wealth ("there is going to be some redistribution"), and education infrastructure. But he concludes that given both the changes the labs say are coming and the grave state of US public opinion, "13-page policy PDFs with no actual commitment or direction ain't it." His standard going forward: every AI industry communication should be judged on whether it reinforces the negative sentiment in the polls or actively combats it.[^openai-proposes-a-new-deal-P_oabCLJhb0]
